# Busted: notes on writing questions

For whoever writes the next deck (German first). `questions.txt` is the
English deck; check any deck with `node scripts/check-questions.mjs <file>`. Write from
scratch for your language and culture: a translated title loses its pun,
and the pun is the prize.

## Vote questions ("Who would most likely …")

The question sets up a small, vivid situation; the title is the punchline
printed after a name on the front page, and later on the player's
magazine cover. Write the title first and ask: would the person grin and
screenshot it? If they would wince, cut it.

- **A situation, not a trait.** "Who would most likely search for their
  phone while talking on it?" beats "Who is the most forgetful?". A scene
  is funny and blameless; a trait is a verdict.
- **Answerable about anyone.** The question is asked about every player,
  including the grandmother and the new coworker. No partners, children,
  pets, cars, jobs or homes assumed ("bossed around by *a* cat", not
  "by their cat"). No gender.
- **Votes should split.** If everyone knows at once who it is, fine; if
  nobody can picture anyone, it is dead. Everyday habits (late, snacks,
  phones, getting lost, singing, sleep) always find a candidate.
- **Mix teasing and honours.** About one in five is a straight compliment
  (best hugs, would survive a zombie apocalypse, hidden talent). It keeps
  the paper from feeling like a list of charges and gives shy players a
  trophy.
- **Title = newspaper honour.** Title Case, ≤ 28 characters, no full stop.
  Vary the shapes; we capped each title word at about 3 uses:
  - office or rank: Chief Hug Officer, Minister of Just in Case,
    Department of Naming Things, Unofficial Mayor of Town
  - award: Sleepyhead of the Year, Inventor of the Century
  - character: Professor Forgetful, Captain Wrong Turn, Agent Fake Name
  - job description: Smoke Alarm Tester, Spice Rack Librarian,
    Vegetable Smuggler, Food Paparazzo
  - pun or twisted phrase: Cereal Offender, Punisher of the Year,
    Hide-and-Sleep Champion, Caught Red-Mouthed, Ghost of Texts Past
  - deadpan fact: Fluent in Dog; Talks Big, Loses Bigger; Grand Exit,
    Take Two
- **Title ≠ question restated.** "Who would get chased by a goose? —
  Goose Chaser" is flat; "Goose Enemy Number One" adds a joke.
- **Theme helps.** The paper is a gossip rag: Chief Gossip Correspondent,
  Busted Deep Scroller, Innocent Until Proven Busted land twice.

## Answer questions ("everyone answers")

The answers are shown anonymously and the room guesses who wrote which.
So the question's job is to make answers *different* and *telling*.

- **Reveal taste, habits or humour.** "What smell instantly makes you
  happy?" gives "fresh bread", "petrol", "my grandma's kitchen": each
  points at someone. "What's the capital of France?" points at nobody.
- **Open, but with a handle.** "What would you name a very fancy
  goldfish?" works because "fancy" pushes everyone towards a joke in their
  own style. "Name a fish" alone gets "Nemo" five times.
- **Answerable in a phone sentence in under a minute.** ≤ 80 characters.
  No stories needing context, no lists, no knowledge or memory tests.
- **No yes/no, no single obvious answer.** Cut: "What's the best pizza
  topping?" (everyone: pepperoni/pineapple war, no reveal), "What would
  you do with a time machine?" (everyone: dinosaurs / lottery; we made it
  "for ten minutes"), "Best invention ever?" (Wi-Fi ×6).
- **Private but sharable.** Party questions dig for small confessions
  ("What have you broken and never admitted to?") that people *want* to
  tell. Never ask for something a person must either lie about or expose
  themselves with (secrets about others, real fears, regrets, body stuff).
- **Watch the template.** "What's the most X thing you've ever done?" and
  "What would you do if you were Y for a day?" are easy to write and blur
  together; we kept only a few of each and rephrased the rest.

## Levels

- **family**: everyone from 8 to 80 at one table, plus coworkers. Silly
  habits, animals, food, gadgets, imagination (dragons, aliens, ants).
  Kids must understand every word; adults should still laugh at the
  title (Cereal Offender works on both levels).
- **party**: friends, teens up. Social embarrassment and small mischief:
  texting an ex, faking calls, lying about the book, group chat drama,
  office passive aggression, reality TV. Still: never sexual, no bodies,
  looks or weight, no money or income, illness, religion, politics, drugs.
  We also left out **alcohol**, since teens play this level.
- The party level is cheekier, not meaner. The test is the same: would
  the winner proudly read it out?

## What we cut, and why

- **Insults wearing a costume:** "Who has the worst taste in music?",
  "Who would be eaten first in a zombie movie?", "Who would fall for every
  prank?". The winner is a victim, not a star. Flipped to "Prank
  Mastermind" instead.
- **Body-adjacent:** sneezes, farts, haircuts, selfies, eating contests,
  gym, tattoos. Fun for some, hurtful for someone else.
- **Assumptions:** "their car", "their pet", "their partner", "call the
  teacher 'Mom'". Rewritten or dropped.
- **Near twins:** we had snooze button *and* sleeping through the day,
  three indecision questions (menu, streaming, sandwich), three clumsiness
  ones, two "secretly a superhero/spy", two wrong-recipient messages.
  Kept the best of each family.
- **Money:** lottery, haggling, shopping sprees, "what would you buy".
- **Mild meanness at work:** "make up gossip to see how fast it spreads"
  sounded like an accusation with a boss in the room; became "narrate
  other people's drama like a sports commentator".
- **Language traps (English):** spellings that differ UK/US (colour,
  cancelled, neighbour, pyjamas) and words that do (football, chips,
  holiday). German has its own: regional words (Brötchen/Semmel), Du/Sie
  (use du throughout), gendered forms in titles (see below).

## Pitfalls for German titles

- Titles must fit after any name: prefer gender-neutral forms or
  constructions ("Schlafmütze des Jahres", "Beauftragte*r" is clumsy;
  nouns like "Legende", "Ikone", "Ministerium für …", "Profi",
  "Weltmeisterschaft im …", "Ehrenmitglied" work for everyone).
- German compounds make great titles ("Snackdetektiv" is gendered;
  "Snack-Spürnase" is not). 28 characters fill up fast: check them.
- Puns on German idioms and party culture beat anything translated.

## Ten good vote questions

1. sleep through an entire day — **Sleepyhead of the Year**: the classic
   shape, sets the tone, everyone has a candidate.
2. eat breakfast cereal at midnight — **Cereal Offender**: a pun adults
   love, harmless for kids.
3. give a name to every gadget they own — **Department of Naming
   Things**: absurd bureaucracy as an honour.
4. search for their phone while talking on it — **Seeker of the
   Obvious**: a universal scene, a grand title.
5. fall asleep in their hiding spot during hide-and-seek —
   **Hide-and-Sleep Champion**: the pun *is* the situation.
6. scream at a spider and then say sorry to it — **Spider Diplomat**:
   two beats (fear, kindness) make a character.
7. give the best hugs — **Chief Hug Officer**: a pure compliment with a
   corporate wink; works with coworkers.
8. text their ex at 2 a.m. — **Ghost of Texts Past**: party embarrassment
   in a literary pun, not a judgement.
9. like a ten-year-old photo while scrolling someone's profile —
   **Busted Deep Scroller**: everyone fears it; it uses the game's name.
10. gossip about someone standing right behind them — **Caught
    Red-Mouthed**: on-theme, a twisted idiom, laughs instead of shame.

## Ten good answer questions

1. What would be your most useless superpower? — instantly funny, every
   answer shows a sense of humour.
2. What smell instantly makes you happy? — sincere, personal, varied.
3. What's the strangest thing you believed as a kid? — tiny stories only
   that person could tell.
4. What small thing annoys you way more than it should? — friends know
   each other's pet peeves: very guessable.
5. What's the most useless thing in your pockets or bag right now? — a
   fact, not a joke; the room recognises the owner.
6. What would you name a very fancy goldfish? — "fancy" gives a handle;
   answers split by humour style.
7. What headline would you love to see about yourself tomorrow? — on
   theme, reveals ambitions and jokes alike.
8. What's your secret move to get the last piece of food? — party-level
   mischief everyone has, written in their own voice.
9. What would a one-star review of your home say? — self-roast with an
   easy format; flatmates will spot each other.
10. What would the gossip pages say about you this week? — the game's own
    premise; invites bragging, confession or nonsense.

## German deck

`questions.de.txt` (373: vote 125 family / 64 party, answer 120 / 64) is
written from scratch, not translated; about a third of the situations
match English ones because they are universal (late, phones, snacks), but
every title was found in German first. Check it with
`node scripts/check-questions.mjs data/questions.de.txt` (the `.de.` in
the name, or `--lang de`, switches to German rules: no Title Case, „…“ in
pairs, no straight `"`, no `:in`/`*in`/`_in`/`/in`/`-In`, articles and
prepositions lowercase inside titles, a W-word in every answer question,
du not Sie, a list of regional words; possibly gendered title nouns warn).

- **Idioms beat inventions.** The best German titles bend a fixed phrase:
  "Alte Liebe rostet nicht", "Nah am Werbewasser gebaut", "Auf frischem
  Tratsch ertappt", "Schlagfertig mit Verspätung", "Vom Hölzchen aufs
  Stöckchen". German has fewer pun-friendly homophones than English, so
  twisted idioms, deadpan statements ("Eine Schraube bleibt übrig") and
  bureaucracy ("Bundesamt für alle Fälle", "Standesamt für Elektrogeräte")
  do the work English does with puns.
- **Gender strategy.** Agent nouns (-er, -meister, -detektiv, -held) are
  masculine and their -in forms feminine, so titles avoid them. Instead:
  animal and thing epithets that German already uses for anyone
  (Siebenschläfer, Naschkatze, Kichererbse, Wetterfrosch, Friedenstaube,
  Wasserratte, Schlitzohr, Goldstück, Stimmungskanone, Rampensau), neuter or
  feminine nouns that apply to everyone (Genie, Phantom, Orakel, Legende,
  Ikone, Lexikon), institutions ("Chefredaktion Gerüchteküche",
  "Inoffizielles Stadtoberhaupt", "Ministerium für Umarmungen"), verb
  phrases ("Spricht fließend Hund", "Hat sich verplappert", "Fragt Löcher
  in den Bauch") and abbreviations ("Prof. Dr. Vergesslich", "Dr. h. c. der
  Ausreden"). "Siebenschläfer" is the dormouse, not a sleeper, so the
  checker allows it. Questions avoid "sein/ihr": "das eigene Handy", "die
  Person", infinitives ("beim Telefonieren").
- **Regional words avoided:** Brötchen/Semmel/Weggli, Sahne/Obers/Rahm,
  Pfannkuchen, Kekse/Plätzchen/Guetzli, Eis/Glace, Tüte/Sackerl,
  Schrank/Kasten, Sessel, Treppe/Stiege, Fahrrad/Velo, grillen/grillieren,
  Fasching/Fasnacht/Karneval (→ Kostümparty), Urlaub/Ferien (→ Reise),
  Abitur/Matura and school grades (1 is best in DE/AT, 6 in CH), month
  names (Jänner), brands (Google, WhatsApp, Haribo) and German-only TV.
  "Achterbahn" (AT: Hochschaubahn) and ß stay: both are read everywhere.
- **Cut:** "Pünktlich wie die Bahn" (praise in Switzerland), Schildbürger
  (a fool), Inselbegabung (medical), Tratschtante (gendered), restating titles.

Eight good vote questions:
1. einen ganzen Tag verschlafen — **Siebenschläfer des Jahres**
2. über absolut nichts stolpern — **Hat die Schwerkraft im Abo**
3. im unpassendsten Moment einen Kicheranfall — **Kichererbse vom Dienst**
4. Möbel ohne Anleitung aufbauen — **Eine Schraube bleibt übrig**
5. den neuesten Klatsch als Erstes hören — **Chefredaktion Gerüchteküche**
6. um zwei Uhr nachts einer alten Liebe schreiben — **Alte Liebe rostet nicht**
7. bei einem Werbespot weinen — **Nah am Werbewasser gebaut**
8. lästern, während die Person dahinter steht — **Auf frischem Tratsch ertappt**

Eight good answer questions: Was hast du als Kind felsenfest geglaubt,
obwohl es gar nicht stimmt? · Welche Kleinigkeit nervt dich viel mehr, als
sie sollte? · Welches schöne Wort benutzt heute kaum noch jemand außer dir?
· Welche Schlagzeile über dich würdest du morgen gern im Busted-Boten
lesen? · Welchen Spruch aus deiner Kindheit sagst du inzwischen selbst? ·
Was ist deine faulste Ausrede, um nicht beim Umzug zu helfen? · Welchen
Spitznamen hattest du früher, den hier niemand kennt? · Wovon tust du so,
als hättest du Ahnung, obwohl du keine hast?
